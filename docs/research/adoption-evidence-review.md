# Evidence favours accurate listings over clever names

No study has tested whether a culturally or locally resonant name, culturally flavoured listing copy, or taste-targeted promotion gets a shelter animal adopted sooner, and the nearest evidence points to effects that are small, absent or confounded. Where real adoptions were measured, the choice of name moved length of stay by nothing (UK cats) or by about 1.5 days (one confounded Ohio dog study), and listing wording by roughly 1 to 3 days, against 10 to 40 days for age, breed type, size and health, which no listing changes. The largest modifiable effects come from getting animals physically seen by more people (kennel viewing, outings, foster networks, fee-waived events), but that evidence is observational, and the only study linking social-media engagement to adoption found more likes going with slightly *lower* odds of adoption. In the United States the binding constraint is not awareness: it is large adult dogs in government shelters, held back by housing rules, cost and handling confidence. AI bio writers are already free and widespread, and none has outcome evidence. Your tool is defensible as a fast way to give every animal a competent, accurate, named listing and a practical exposure plan, and as the first instrument that could run the randomised test nobody has run; it is not defensible as a way to put "the right animal in front of the right people" or to shorten stays. The main risk of harm runs through inflated expectations: in a UK cohort of about 4,500 adopters, finding a dog more work than expected carried about ten times the odds of return, and a model writing warm copy from sparse facts invents exactly the traits that open that gap. This review ran under access limits (blocked publisher pages, an exhausted search budget), so several figures are flagged as abstract-only, secondary or unverified, and some topics were never searched; the final section lists them.

## Age, breed type and health shift stays by weeks; names and wording by days

Three conventions apply throughout. "Actual" means a recorded adoption, length of stay (LOS) or return; "clicks" means online engagement; "stated" means survey ratings or intentions, which this literature shows are a poor guide to behaviour. A hazard ratio (HR) of 0.5 means the animal is adopted at half the daily rate of the comparison group. Figures tagged *abstract only* or *secondary summary* were not read in the full paper; figures tagged *recalled* come from a researcher's memory of a published abstract and were not re-read during this review, so check them at the DOI before quoting.

### What a shelter cannot change

Almost all of this evidence is observational analysis of shelter records. It is consistent across countries for age, size, breed type and health, and weak or contradictory for coat colour and sex.

These traits shift averages by days to weeks, yet they explain little of why one animal waits longer than another. In the New York no-kill data, mean LOS was 34.6 days with a standard deviation of 48.7, so the spread within any group dwarfs the 10 to 20 day gaps between groups ([Brown 2013](https://faunalytics.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/Citation2222_Effects%20of%20Phenotypic%20Characteristics%20on%20the%20Length%20of%20Stay%20of%20Dogs%20at%20Two%20No%20Kill%20Animal%20Shelters.pdf)). A machine-learning model on **114,256 US shelter records** sorted animals into four LOS bands with only about 60% precision, working from structured attributes such as age, size and colour with no name or description text at all ([Rajendran & Bradley 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12917-020-02728-2)). Region mattered more than any trait: dogs in northern and western US shelters were adopted at 3.1 and 3.8 times the daily rate of dogs in southern shelters ([Cain 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/10/11/1959)). That unexplained remainder leaves room for other influences, but it is not evidence that marketing fills it; shelter, season, capacity and chance all sit in the same residual. A systematic review of 18 studies concluded that a "black dog syndrome" does not appear universally and that age, breed and size are the consistent predictors ([Díaz Videla 2020](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/3334/333468469005/333468469005.pdf)). No meta-analysis of any LOS predictor was found.

Two patterns matter for your tool. First, **no modifiable lever has a replicated, multi-site, randomised effect on actual adoption**. Six practices have experimental evidence and they split between positive and null; the largest headline numbers all come from designs with obvious selection or confounding. Second, the interventions that change how many people *encounter* an animal (events, kennel viewing, outings, foster networks) show the largest associations, while interventions that change how the animal looks or behaves in its kennel often change behaviour without changing adoption. Marketing belongs to the encounter category, which is encouraging, but that category also has the weakest methods. Intrinsic traits and listing features both act at the browsing stage, where breed and appearance dominate; once a dog was out of the kennel meeting a visitor, breed, age, size and colour stopped predicting adoption and the dog's behaviour toward the person took over, in 250 observed meetings at one Florida shelter ([Protopopova & Wynne 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2014.04.007)). One paper that could change the fee row was located but could not be read: a study of adoption discounts and LOS for dogs at a municipal shelter in New South Wales ([PMC12837617](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12837617)).

Two disagreements in the research notes need flagging. For the Florida breed-label removal, one set of notes gave LOS falling "from 42.07 to about 26 days" and another "by only 1.5 days". The 42.07 figure is the mean from the separate 30-dog Arizona comparison, so the first reading is a conflation. A re-check of the paper's text during writing confirmed the 42.07 vs 12.80 comparison and the 52% to 64% adoption change, and found that the before/after LOS means sit in a table the page reader could not extract; the paper's text says only that removal "significantly reduced" stay ([Gunter 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0146857)). Treat the size of the LOS change in days as unconfirmed. For the foster-ambassador study, three readings of the same table gave intake-to-adoption means of 41.5 vs 20.4 days, about 52 vs 26, and 47.4 vs 12.3; the direction and significance (p < .001) are not in doubt, the exact days are.

## Names, wording and photos: three listing levers, none proven

### Names: one null, one confounded association, no test of local resonance

Beyond the Rix study you hold, **exactly one peer-reviewed study with an actual-adoption outcome was found, and it is observational**. Quadlin and Montgomery analysed 1,636 dogs at one county shelter in Columbus, Ohio, with each name rated by about 50 online respondents. Names perceived as White were associated with faster adoption (about 7.6 days for a name rated 0% White vs about 6 days for one rated 90% White), names perceived as nonhuman ("Fluffy"-type) with adoption about 1.5 days slower, and Black-perceived names with a penalty concentrated in pit bulls: about 13.5 vs 9 days, a 4.5-day gap ([Quadlin & Montgomery 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01902725221090900)). The authors had no data on what the dogs looked like, and staff assigned the names, so the name could mark how staff perceived the dog instead of causing the delay. Stays at that shelter ran 6 to 13 days, so the effect would be trivial for long-stay animals elsewhere. The two real-outcome studies also disagree: nonhuman names were slower in Ohio dogs, while name category, human names included, made no difference in UK cats.

**Nobody has tested culturally or locally resonant names against anything.** The closest evidence is that two of Rix's ten name categories were "fictional characters" and "Disney names", and category had no effect on LOS, which is weak evidence against a pop-culture benefit and says nothing about names matched to a specific local audience ([Rix 2021](https://rvc-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1443654)). A Crossref keyword search for name and adoption studies since 2010 returned only Quadlin and Rix. No experiment with random name assignment exists for either species, and no study measures clicks or enquiries by name. Everything else is practitioner anecdote: staff at several US shelters say silly or themed names "catch the eye", with no counts or comparison groups ([TODAY](https://www.today.com/pets/pets/creative-dog-names-animal-shelters-help-pets-get-adopted-rcna30332)), while the Humane Society's trade magazine says "most adopters don't choose a pet based solely on their name, and many eventually rename" ([HumanePro](https://humanepro.org/magazine/articles/spilling-kibble-how-do-you-choose-names-animals)). No figure for how many adopters rename was found. Shelters already do your naming feature by hand: Kansas City's shelter posted a cat named "Catrick Mahomes", and the ASPCA's naming tips recommend themes and favourite characters, citing no outcome data ([OK Whatever](https://www.okwhatever.org/topics/wtf/rescue-dog-names-celebrities); [ASPCApro](https://www.aspcapro.org/resource/five-tips-naming-animals-intake)).

Open-data analyses add nothing reliable. Blog analyses of Austin's public data disagree on whether unnamed animals do worse (unnamed dogs left *faster*, 7 vs 27 days, in one), because a missing name marks animals never processed for adoption: reclaimed strays, neonatal kittens, quick transfers ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/arogya01/its-not-the-coat-beige-dog-bureau-2f6i)). The peer-reviewed PetFinder.my model names age, breed, number of animals in the listing and sterilisation as key predictors, and not the name ([Zadeh 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0957417422009083)).

Adjacent psychology gives a plausible story and several warnings. Easy-to-pronounce human names were rated more favourably across five studies ([Laham 2012](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/898w6/the-name-pronunciation-effect-why-people-like-mr-smith-more-than-mr-colquhoun)), which argues for short, fluent names and against obscure character names and puns that need decoding. The claim that people choose things resembling their own names in real life was traced largely to cohort, geographic and ethnic confounds ([Simonsohn 2011](https://repository.upenn.edu/oid_papers/233)). And an archival study of a century of first names found that names which rose fastest were abandoned fastest, with parents avoiding them as fads ([Berger & Le Mens 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0812647106), *recalled*); a name drawn from whatever is spiking locally is a fast-riser by construction. The demonstrated name effects are asymmetric: stereotype-laden labels hurt measurably, while the upside of a "good" name is small and shown mainly on rating scales.

### Listing text: real-outcome studies favour plain facts, surveys favour emotion

The single most important pattern in this literature is a split between what people say moves them and what is associated with adoption. Stated-intention studies favour emotional, anthropomorphic and loss-framed text; the few actual-outcome studies (including the Rix and Markowitz papers you hold) favour factual, third-person text. Survey findings should not be used to justify emotive copy.

On humour and pop-culture references, **no controlled or observational study exists**. The evidence is viral anecdote: "Prancer", a Chihuahua advertised in a brutally honest comic listing in 2021, was adopted within about a week of going viral ([NBC Chicago](https://www.nbcchicago.com/news/national-international/prancer-the-demonic-chihuahua-that-went-viral-finds-his-forever-home/2495235/)). Such cases are selected on the outcome, and Prancer worked through candour about a difficult dog written by someone who knew him, which is a different intervention from an AI inserting local references for an animal it has not met. In commercial advertising, a meta-analysis of 369 correlations found humour raises attention and liking but "significantly reduces source credibility" ([Eisend 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-008-0096-y)), and a shelter's credibility is part of what an adopter is relying on. Nothing was found on urgency wording ("last chance", euthanasia-list pleas) against any outcome, and nothing isolating restriction phrases against enquiries or adoption.

The text effects that exist are small: about 2.5 days for voice in UK cats, against a 12-day gap between young-adult and senior cats in the same model. The largest text effect on record is for a single label word, "pit bull". Because descriptions reflect the real animal, the observational finding in your Archer and Nakamura papers that negative behavioural words go with slower adoption does not show that omitting them would help.

### Photos gate the click, but most photo advice rests on nulls

The headline number in this literature, that a great photo gets a dog adopted 29 days faster, comes from one observational study of 468 already-adopted black Labrador mixes, and applies to young dogs only (14 vs 43 days); among adults the only clear difference was outdoor vs indoor photos (37 vs 51 days). Bandanas, visible tongues, people, toys and cage settings had no effect ([Lampe & Witte 2015, abstract record](https://rvc-repository.worktribe.com/output/1403487); [secondary summary](https://www.companionanimalpsychology.com/2015/07/great-photos-are-important-to-dog.html)). The research notes did not conflict on these figures, but nobody could open the full text: the day figures come from a reputable secondary summary, corroborated by a radio interview with the first author ([Texas Standard](https://texasstandard.org/stories/glamour-shots-help-shelter-pets-find-homes-faster/)). Because rescues that take good photos differ in other ways, 29 days is an upper bound on a causal effect, not an estimate of one. The statistic is also widely misused to promote professional photography, which the study did not test.

For cats, the only photographer-controlled feature associated with more Petfinder clicks was a toy in the frame; framing, angle, ear position and eye contact did nothing. Cats with more than one click a day were adopted in about 9 days vs 23, but coat colour and age drove both clicks and speed (892 cats, one New York shelter; [abstract](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de67e74c5aef494fdbef3b); day figures from a [press summary](https://psmag.com/social-justice/all-of-those-cat-photos-on-the-internet-are-actually-doing-some-good/)). Background, the best-studied attribute, is contradictory across four studies: outdoors better in Lampe and Witte, kennel shots fastest in your Nakamura 2020 paper, and no effect on clicks in the one controlled experiment, where 680 participants saw the same dogs digitally placed on four backgrounds (clicks 3.8% to 5.2%, p = .314) ([Lamb 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0255551)). A machine-learning dataset of 9,912 PetFinder.my photos found that hand-labelled checklist items (face visible, in focus, human present) barely predicted page traffic ([Pawpularity, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.06061v2)).

Video has no evidence on actual adoption. Two stated-perception experiments found small favourable shifts: dogs on 30-second video were rated friendlier and more trainable (735 participants, UK; [Pyzer 2017](https://pure.hartpury.ac.uk/en/publications/effects-of-video-footage-versus-photographs-on-perception-of-dog-/)), and cats on 10-second video scored 0.18 to 0.30 points higher on a 5-point adoptability scale, a difference the authors judged too small to justify the effort ([Schoenfeld-Tacher 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2019.00087/full)). Short-form video, livestreams and playgroup clips were not searched.

**No study partitions adoption outcomes between photo and text.** The defensible model is two-stage: the photo, and specifically the face, decides whether a browser clicks (95% of photo presentations in the Lamb experiment produced no click), and text then shapes the judgement of those who do. In your Archer paper, which photo was shown explained 6% of the variance in ratings and differences between raters 44%. A text-only tool works on the second stage and has no influence on the first. On AI image editing there is no evidence of benefit and a documented cost: in March 2026 Los Angeles County's animal agency warned the public that AI-altered animal images misrepresent temperament and listed "overly perfect expressions" and "blurred edges" as red flags ([LA County DACC](https://animalcare.lacounty.gov/news/ai-altered-animal-images-pose-public-safety-risk-dacc-warns/)).

## Exposure and people move animals; taste targeting has never been tested

### Promotion: large associations for being seen, nothing for whom you target

No study measures, with a comparison group, the effect of posting a specific animal on Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Nextdoor or X on that animal's LOS or adoption odds. The one peer-reviewed study linking engagement to an adoption indicator analysed 592 Facebook posts from 13 North Carolina shelters: **each additional like went with slightly lower odds of the animal being marked adopted (odds ratio 0.995, p = 0.02)**, on weak data, since only 108 posts had a known outcome and adoptions were not verified ([Morrison 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/14/22/3217)). The likely reason is that sympathetic long-stay cases attract likes from people who will never adopt. Engagement is therefore not a usable proxy for the outcome you care about.

No experiment has tested paid or targeted advertising for shelter animals against adoptions. What exists measures reach and recall: the Ad Council's 2015 Facebook campaign reached 8.66 million people and lifted ad recall from 41% to 56%, with no adoption outcome ([Shorty Awards entry](https://shortyawards.com/8th/the-shelter-pet-project)). A vendor case study reported an "86% lift in conversion" from targeting the household and social connections of website visitors instead of the visitors alone, with "conversion" undefined ([The Drum](https://www.thedrum.com/news/ad-council-leverages-audience-data-give-pets-new-home)). Pedigree's "Adoptable" campaign in New Zealand geo-targeted ads featuring individual local shelter dogs and claimed half the featured dogs were adopted within two weeks, with no comparison group ([Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-makers/global-ads-spotlight-when-ai-met-animal-welfare-in-a-pedigree-campaign-to-boost-pet-adoptions-65100.htm)). That campaign targeted who is near the dog, not what the neighbourhood likes.

For off-site events, pet-store centres and mega-adoption campaigns, the evidence addresses retention and not speed. Adopters from in-store centres and events kept animals at the same rate as shelter adopters, about four in five after a year (*secondary summary*; [Neidhart & Boyd 2002](https://faunalytics.org/companion-animal-adoption-study/)), and off-site adoption did not survive screening as a return risk among 6,212 Queensland dog adoptions ([Thumpkin 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/12/19/2568)). Volume claims (more than 1 million adoptions for Clear the Shelters since 2015) are unaudited and have no counterfactual ([Clear the Shelters](https://www.cleartheshelters.com/)). About a third of adopters at Maddie's fee-waived events were adopting from a shelter for the first time, the best hint that publicised events reach new people, though publicity and price cannot be separated ([Maddie's Fund 2013 survey](https://www.maddiesfund.org/assets/documents/Grant%20Giving/adoption%20Days%20Survey%20Summary%20_FINAL.pdf)). For partnerships with local businesses and media, everything found is anecdote: an Alabama shelter manager reports "about a 90% adoption rate" for newspaper-featured dogs, with no baseline and with the featured dogs also receiving training ([Cullman Tribune](https://www.cullmantribune.com/?p=95001)). Brewery tie-ins, pizza-box flyers, workplace fostering and mobile units were not searched.

The best-evidenced finding on "where and to whom" is the foster-ambassador study. Dogs marketed by foster carers through their own networks reached a different adopter pool. In New Orleans their adopters lived further from the shelter (mean 14.3 km vs 8.5 km), and only 29% of ambassador adopters had previously had a pet from a shelter or rescue. In Charleston 54% found the dog online, 18% through a friend and 15% by seeing it in public in an "Adopt Me" vest, whereas **81% of ordinary in-shelter adopters first met their dog by walking in** ([Mohan-Gibbons 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0091959)). Returns were lower, and adoption was slower. The working channel was a person who knew the dog telling people who knew that person: network targeting, not taste targeting. Surveys agree that word of mouth leads as the way people hear about shelters (41%), ahead of social media (34%), shelter websites (32%), local events (19%) and local news (17%) ([Hill's 2025](https://theaawa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Hills-Shelter-Report-2025.pdf)).

On localised or culturally tailored marketing, no study shows that tailoring to a community's language, culture or interests changes adoption outcomes. Two bilingual events in south Texas drew over 3,000 people and "nearly 200" adoptions, self-reported and with no baseline ([Best Friends](https://bestfriends.org/stories/features/bilingual-outreach-results-200-pet-adoptions-south-texas)). A Los Angeles survey of 283 residents in high-intake, mostly Hispanic ZIP codes found Spanish speakers were less likely to have seen foster advertising at all and preferred text, email and post to social media ([McDonald 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fvets.2022.897687/full)), which is a finding about access and trust and not about taste. Where evidence exists on who adopts, the gradients are distance, income and age: 13,837 Toronto adopters clustered around the shelter ([Toronto Humane Society study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fvets.2021.785071/full)), and 21,270 animals in British Columbia flowed from more vulnerable to less vulnerable communities ([Ly 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fvets.2021.784389/full)).

Whether spotlighting one animal adds an adoption or moves an adopter from another animal is unmeasured. US dog adoptions in the first half of 2024 were 5% below both 2023 and 2019 ([Shelter Animals Count](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Dogs-and-Data-Adopters-Space-Crisis.pdf)), so displacement is a live possibility; against that, the Florida label change coincided with higher adoption across all breed groups, so the adopter pool is not strictly fixed. The only long-stay promotion pilot found covered 20 dogs with no comparison group ([Maddie's Fund](https://chewonthis.maddiesfund.org/2017/10/long-stay-dogs/)). Timing is the one promotion fact with data behind it: organisations open on Saturday record 61% more adoptions that day than on an average weekday ([Shelter Animals Count 2025](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/reducing-barriers-and-expanding-access-to-improve-adoption-outcomes/)), and Shelterluv data show 55% of adoptions happen between 5 and 8 pm ([SAC webinar recap](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/webinar-recap-mid-year-momentum/)).

### What marketing science predicts for taste targeting: small effects, hard to detect

The research outside shelters supports "culture shifts which type of pet people want, slowly, at population scale" and does not test "a cultural reference on one animal's listing makes it leave sooner". Films featuring a breed were followed by rises in US kennel-club registrations for up to ten years, an effect that has declined since the early twentieth century ([Ghirlanda 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0106565)). The closest analogue to your tool is a study of *Finding Dory* that measured the whole funnel: Google searches for the fish rose to 2.1 times the expected level and returned to normal within about two months, while imports were 0.8 times expected and aquarium visits unchanged ([Veríssimo 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13280-019-01233-7)). **A pop-culture hook produced a burst of attention and no change in acquisition.** The same sceptical standard cuts against the harm story too: the claim that Harry Potter caused an owl-dumping wave found no support in UK trade data ([Megias 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0182368)).

Tailoring effects in large trials are real and small. A meta-analysis of 57 tailored health-message studies found r = .074, using data collected about each individual recipient ([Noar 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.4.673), *figures recalled*); a meta-analysis of cultural tailoring found about r = .05, larger for values-based tailoring than for surface tailoring such as names and familiar references ([Huang & Shen 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12243), *figures recalled and less certain*). A pop-culture name is surface tailoring. The most favourable large synthesis found message matching improved persuasion at about r = .20, again matching on measured individual motives ([Joyal-Desmarais 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000377), *recalled*). For language models specifically, matched ChatGPT messages scored higher on stated ratings in seven small experiments (total N = 1,788; [Matz 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-53755-0)), while a pre-registered study of about 8,600 people found microtargeted GPT-4 messages no more persuasive than generic ones ([Hackenburg & Margetts 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2403116121), *recalled*). All of the supportive studies share three things your tool lacks: individual-level data on each recipient, the ability to send different messages to different people, and paid delivery at scale. A volunteer posts one listing to whoever the platform shows it to.

Effects this small are also hard to see. Across 25 large advertising experiments, sales were so noisy that the median confidence interval on return was over 100 percentage points wide ([Lewis & Rao 2015](https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2015-lewis.pdf)), and in 15 Facebook experiments observational comparisons were wrong by a factor of three or more half the time ([Gordon 2019](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/gordon_b/files/fb_comparison.pdf)). A researcher's illustrative arithmetic, built on assumed numbers, puts the gain from perfectly executed tailoring at about 0.6 of an extra enquiry per animal for a post seen by 1,000 local people. Early success stories from pilot shelters will be uninformative, and "our promoted animals went faster" comparisons will flatter the tool.

The data source has its own validity problem. **No independent validation of Qloo's affinity scores at any geography was found**, and Qloo's developer documentation was blocked to the researchers, so its sources and granularity are unrecorded here. A comparable product, Esri's neighbourhood segmentation, states that area data "cannot be used to infer information about individuals" ([Esri](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/esri-demographics/latest/esri-demographics/tapestry-segmentation.htm)). Three inferences follow, none tested. "Unusually popular here" is an over-index statistic, which surfaces niche items: something three times as popular as the national average can still be known to a few percent of residents, so most viewers would see only an odd name. The shelter's adopters are not the residents of its neighbourhood, since adopters travel and are a selected subset. And cultural tastes are strong statistical proxies for race, religion and politics ([Kosinski 2013](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1218772110), *recalled*), so "target fans of X in area Y" is partly demographic targeting under another name, with the risk of narrowing who gets approached or of producing racially coded names of the kind penalised in Ohio.

### Matching: the one controlled evaluation found nothing

The positive results for the ASPCA's Meet Your Match programme (returns down by a third to a half, adoptions up 14% to 38% at named shelters) come from the ASPCA's own unpublished, uncontrolled before-and-after reports ([ASPCApro](https://www.aspcapro.org/node/103725)). The one independent, controlled evaluation, a five-year interrupted time series for cats at Seattle's shelter against a comparison shelter, found **no change in adoptions, LOS, returns or euthanasia**, and found the programme was delivered without error for 3 of 183 assessed cats, 1.6% ([Darling 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/13/17/2752/htm)). The null may reflect failed delivery, which is itself the lesson for a volunteer-run tool: a multi-step protocol collapsed in real use, as the structured meet-and-greet did. The underlying assessments predict little; the cat assessment correlated 0.30 and 0.18 with behaviour at home ([Weiss 2015](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/5/1/71/htm)), and a food-guarding test was right for 55% of positives while 22% of negatives guarded at home ([Marder 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168159113001810)).

The only multi-shelter study of matching practice, a survey of 370 US organisations, associated better return and euthanasia figures with talking to adopters before they see any dogs and showing only good-fit dogs (*abstract only*, no effect sizes; [Reese 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33375872/)). That is restricting choice for an individual, the opposite of widening an animal's audience. No study of any design tests matching by shared cultural interests or perceived similarity, and no outcome evidence was found for consumer matching quizzes or the natural-language adoption search Amazon launched with Best Friends in April 2026 ([Best Friends](https://bestfriends.org/who-we-are/media/amazons-ai-powered-tool-matches-dogs-and-cats-potential-adopters)). Stated preferences are diverse: a survey of 517 potential adopters put behaviour first (58.9%), ahead of age, size and lifestyle fit ([Minnis 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36324195/)).

### Returns follow expectation gaps, and overselling is the plausible harm

Baseline return rates in single-shelter US data run about **10% to 18% for dogs and 4% to 12% for cats**: 16.3% and 4.2% within six months among 23,932 adoptions in Charleston ([Powell 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87649-2)), and 11.7% of 2,642 cats followed for up to four years, half of them returned after the first 30 days ([Mundschau & Suchak 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/13/2/243/htm)). The best-replicated risk factors are on the animal side: adult dogs had 3.4 times the odds of return of puppies and adult cats 4.1 times those of kittens; dogs of 45 kg or more were readmitted at 2.2 times the rate of small dogs in Queensland ([Thumpkin 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/12/19/2568)); severe resource guarders were returned 40% of the time vs 17.5% ([McGuire 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912206)); cats with a bite on record had 4.4 times the return risk ([Mundschau & Suchak 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/13/2/243/htm)). Behaviour is the leading stated reason for dog returns (36%), followed by conflict with existing pets (18%). A tool that speeds adoption of large adult dogs will, other things equal, place animals from the highest-return groups, so returns per adoption can rise even if the tool does nothing wrong.

**No study directly tests whether flattering, sanitised or inaccurate listings raise returns.** The case rests on expectations. In a prospective UK cohort of Dogs Trust adopters (about 4,500 respondents), owners who found the dog more work than expected had 9.9 times the odds of return (95% CI 4.1–24.6) ([Diesel 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18243374/)). In a small US prospective study (132 adopters, 29 returns), those who later returned dogs had started with higher expectations of the dog's behaviour and of the bond ([Powell 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/12/9/1053/htm)). A listing that frames a dog through a beloved character invites the adopter to expect the character. A return also costs the animal: returned adult dogs had 3.9 times the odds of euthanasia of returned puppies, and pit-bull-type dogs 2.6 times, even though 80% of returned dogs were re-adopted ([Powell 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87649-2)).

Disclosure evidence cuts both ways, and the dividing line is accuracy. Labels of doubtful accuracy hurt animals without protecting adopters: across nine US shelters and 14,180 dogs, dogs labelled food guarders stayed longer (13.6 vs 9.9 days) and were euthanised more (15.4% vs 10.9%), and dropping the test did not raise returns among those dogs (9% vs 11%), though returns for all dogs rose from 10% to 13% in the second period for reasons the authors do not explain ([Mohan-Gibbons 2018](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/8/2/27/htm)). Shelters also cannot verify much of what listings assert: staff named the DNA-predominant breed for 56.7% of 919 dogs ([Gunter 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0202633)). Real, serious problems are different: severe guarding and bite history carry two to four times the return risk, and California law requires written disclosure of a dog's known bite history before adoption ([Cal. FAC § 30526](https://california.public.law/codes/food_and_agricultural_code_section_30526)).

The common fear that light-hearted, impulsive or promotion-driven adoption leads to more returns is not supported, though the evidence is mostly self-report. Six-month retention did not differ between adopters who researched heavily and those who decided on the spot (572 adopters, six US shelters; [American Humane 2013](https://www.americanhumane.org/app/uploads/2016/08/petsmart-keeping-pets-phase-ii.pdf)), free and low-cost cat adopters showed no attachment or retention penalty in several surveys ([Zito 2015](https://www.g2z.org.au/pdf/FINAL%20Animals%20paper.pdf)), and no study measures returns for adoptions triggered by a specific campaign, pop-culture tie-in or themed name. The risk in your design is wrong expectations, not frivolity.

## The US bottleneck is large dogs in government shelters, and bio-writing is already free

### Scale: intake is falling, outflow of big dogs is the problem

Two modelled national series exist and they differ mainly in coverage. Shelter Animals Count (now a programme of the ASPCA) includes about 9,400 rescues; Best Friends counts about 3,900 to 4,000 brick-and-mortar shelters.

| 2025, dogs and cats | Shelter Animals Count (shelters and rescues) | Best Friends (shelters only) |
|---|---|---|
| Intake | 5.8 million (3.0 million cats, 2.8 million dogs), down 2% | About 4.7 million (2.4 million dogs, 2.3 million cats) |
| Adoptions | 4.2 million (2.2 million cats, 2.0 million dogs) | About 2.5 million |
| Euthanised or killed | 597,000 euthanised; 757,000 non-live outcomes in all | 396,000; save rate 82.5% |

Sources: [Shelter Animals Count 2025](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/2025-report/); [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/about-us/press-releases/shelter-animals-count-releases-2025-annual-data-report-marking-10-years); [Best Friends 2025](https://bestfriends.org/network/studies-publications/shelter-pet-lifesaving-data-2025-report). Neither source reconciles the gap in deaths, and neither series has published peer-reviewed validation; the Shelter Animals Count annual report PDF could not be opened, so intake by type and species is missing here.

Intake is not the growing pressure. Dog intake is about 14% below 2019 ([HumanePro](https://humanepro.org/page/pets-numbers)), and Shelter Animals Count attributes capacity problems to "increased length of stay rather than a surge in intakes", with the widest gap between animals in and animals out at government shelters ([SAC webinar recap](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/webinar-recap-mid-year-momentum/)). Government shelters adopt out about 38% of intake against 80% or more at rescues and private shelters, and account for 59% of non-live outcomes ([SAC organisation types](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/how-organization-type-shapes-shelter-trends-in-2025/); [Shelter Animals Count 2025](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/2025-report/)). For cats the mortality problem is neonatal: kittens under five months were 65% of cat non-live outcomes in the first half of 2025 ([Maddie's Fund summary](https://chewonthis.maddiesfund.org/2025/08/shelter-animals-count-releases-2025-mid-year-report/)), which is a foster and veterinary-capacity problem that no listing helps.

Typical length of stay is short and heavily skewed. Median time from intake to adoption for large dogs fell from 19 to 17 days in 2025, about 70% longer than for small dogs, having been about 12 days in 2019; only those figures and the 2019 row are firm, because machine readings of the chart disagreed on other cells ([Hill's 2026](https://theaawa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Hills-Shelter-Report-2026.pdf)). A snapshot of animals *currently* in care gives far higher numbers, a median of 47 days for dogs and 43 for cats, because long-stayers accumulate ([Shelter Animals Count](https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/the-pressure-of-2025-managing-capacity-through-community-communication-and-collaboration/)). In the one peer-reviewed series, a California county system with over 27,000 dog records had a mean stay of 17.1 days and a 90th percentile of 36 days, and stays rose 37.7% after COVID in a way that animal mix could not explain and that coincided with restricted visitor access ([Mavrovouniotis 2026](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0342102)). No national share of animals staying 30, 60 or 90 days was found, and no national cat LOS by age.

The animals that wait longest, ranked by quality of evidence, are large adult dogs (national data), pit-bull-type dogs (single-shelter studies), seniors and animals with health or behaviour concerns (multiple record studies), and, among cats, seniors, FeLV-positive cats and required pairs. Large dogs are 26% of dog intake but 22% of dog adoptions, and their adoptions fell 9% in the first half of 2025 while small-dog adoptions rose 6%. The stated barriers are not about visibility: among people unlikely to adopt a large dog, the reasons were food cost (43%), transport and physical ability (41% each), veterinary cost and space (36% each), and 68% of all respondents said housing that allows large dogs is harder to find. One figure does favour an information tool: only 40% of those unlikely adopters thought shelters give enough information, against 78% of likely adopters. This comes from a pet-food company's non-probability survey of 2,000 adults ([Hill's 2026](https://theaawa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Hills-Shelter-Report-2026.pdf)).

### An extra day costs $10 to $21 on average, and less at the margin

The best-sourced figures put fully loaded care at **roughly $10 to $21 per animal per day in 2017–2019 dollars**: $12 for cats and $13 for dogs at Austin Pets Alive! in 2018, where stays of four to five months cost over $1,800 per cat and $2,000 per dog ([Maddie's Fund](https://www.maddiesfund.org/estimating-the-cost-to-care-for-animals-at-austin-pets-alive.htm)); $21.06 for dog kennelling and about $13 to $14 for cats in a Washington county study ([Pierce County](https://www.piercecountywa.gov/DocumentCenter/View/74532/Final-Pierce-County-Animal-Services-Report)); and a $10 contract price in an Arkansas city ([Fort Smith audit](https://fortsmithar.gov/home/showpublisheddocument/2470/638231107098970000)). No measured per-day figure after 2019 was found, and a widely quoted "$18 per day" Virginia figure could not be traced to a source. Every one of these is an average cost. Food and consumables for one more day are a few dollars, and staff and buildings are fixed in the short run; when a shelter is full, the real marginal cost of an occupied kennel is the animal that cannot be admitted. A business case belongs in kennel-days freed, with dollars as a secondary illustration. On welfare, hair cortisol in 52 Dutch shelter dogs rose from 16.0 to 21.8 pg/mg after six weeks in the shelter and fell back after adoption ([van der Laan 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-09140-w)); claims of progressive behavioural deterioration rest on weaker cross-sectional evidence.

### New York: crowding and housing, with rescue partners as the main exit for dogs

Animal Care Centers of NYC took in 5,965 dogs and 10,011 cats in 2025. Only 1,728 dogs (29% of intake) were adopted directly, against 2,375 transferred to rescue partners and 1,111 returned to owners; 692 were euthanised. For cats, direct adoption was the largest exit (4,815, or 48%) ([ACC 2025 report](https://www.nycacc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/ACC-2025-EOY-SAC.pdf)). ACC passed 1,000 animals in care for the first time in July 2025 and suspended general intake, saying one in three surrenders that year was due to housing insecurity ([NY1](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/news/2025/07/21/animal-care-centers-of-nyc-suspend-intakes-at-shelters)); a year later it was within about 50 animals of that mark and waived fees for large dogs and adult cats ([Hoodline](https://hoodline.com/2026/07/nyc-shelters-packed-to-the-rafters-as-pets-desperately-need-new-homes/)). New York State as a whole has an 89% save rate ([Daily Gazette](https://www.dailygazette.com/only-21-ny-animal-shelters-are-not-yet-no-kill/article_3b59a6c9-aca8-4a82-b62c-338fc1d20674.html)). For your demo this means the audience for a dog's listing at a New York municipal shelter is as much rescue partners and foster carers as public adopters, and the surrender pressure that fills the kennels is not something marketing addresses. No ACC length-of-stay figure was found.

### Existing tools: AI bio writers are commoditised and unevaluated

Listings are typed once into shelter software (Shelterluv, PetPoint, Pawlytics and others) and syndicated to Petfinder, Adopt a Pet and, through RescueGroups.org, more than 200 sites, so the description field in the shelter system is where text enters the ecosystem ([RescueGroups.org](https://rescuegroups.org/)). Petfinder shut its public API on 2 December 2025 with about 25 days' notice ([Airdrie Media](https://www.airdriemedia.com/petfinder-shuts-down-its-api/)), so building on its data is fragile. Vendor help centres could not be reached, so whether the mainstream systems have quietly added AI features is unconfirmed.

AI bio writing is free and already routine. Shelter staff were sharing ChatGPT for bios in December 2022 ([Maddie's Fund forum](https://forum.maddiesfund.org/discussion/animal-bios-when-theres-no-creative-juice-left-in-the-tank)); PetSmart Charities launched the free Rescue Writer in November 2023; WeRescue Hub has a one-click description button; Animals First integrates GPT-4. **For none of these was any outcome evidence found: no A/B test, pilot data or peer-reviewed evaluation.** Rescue Writer has published no usage or adoption figures in three years and carries the disclaimer that accuracy is the user's responsibility ([rescuewriter.ai](https://rescuewriter.ai/)); a shelter user's complaint is that it "usually adds a fake 'background story' for the pet" ([Maddie's Fund forum](https://forum.maddiesfund.org/discussion/if-you-use-ai-at-all-how-do-you-use-it)). The only claim that an AI bio tool raised adoption rates is an unsourced content-farm article. Sector guidance from HeARTs Speak, ASPCApro and Shelter Animals Count is practitioner opinion that cites no outcome study and converges on "first draft only, a human supplies the facts and edits" ([HeARTs Speak](https://heartsspeak.org/pet-bios-using-chatgpt/)).

Your combination appears unoccupied: a screen of all 344 projects in the 2025 Qloo hackathon gallery by name and tagline found none about pets or shelters, though "taste data into marketing blueprint" is a crowded pattern there ([Qloo hackathon gallery](https://qloo-hackathon.devpost.com/project-gallery)). Novelty is not evidence of effect. Indirect evidence on AI copy is cautionary: labelling a headline as AI-generated lowered accuracy ratings by 0.11 to 0.17 points on a 6-point scale because readers assumed no human supervision ([Altay & Gilardi 2024](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/3/10/pgae403/7795946)), and writers given AI ideas produced stories that were rated better but were more similar to one another ([Doshi & Hauser 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00506)). If several shelters in one city use the same model and the same taste data, the same hit show will name several animals in the same month. A student model that predicted adoption outcomes with 0.66 accuracy on its training shelter scored 0.04 and 0.32 on two other cities without retraining ([UC Berkeley](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/projects/2024/pawpulation-forecast)), a warning that what works in one community needs local validation in the next.

## Verdicts: name unsupported, listing mixed, promotion plan untested

| Feature | Verdict | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| Name drawn from locally popular culture | **Untested** as designed; **unsupported** as a speed lever | No study of locally or culturally resonant names exists. The two real-outcome studies give a null (UK cats, including fictional and Disney categories) and a confounded 1–2 day association (Ohio dogs). The only documented name effect with any size is a penalty for stereotype-coded names on pit bulls. Many adopters rename. Over-indexed local tastes are likely to be niche, and fast-rising names read as fads |
| Short adoption listing | **Mixed** | Supported as coverage and labour-saving: staff report creative fatigue, some description appears better than none (blog-level data), and plain, accurate behaviour information is what adopters ask for. Unsupported as persuasion: wording effects with real outcomes are 1–3 days and observational, no AI-versus-human comparison exists, and "cute" wording did not beat plain wording in the one experiment. Carries a real risk if the model invents traits |
| Promotion plan (where, and to which audiences) | **Untested** for taste-based targeting; **mixed** for exposure in general | No study tests audience selection by cultural taste, and tested gradients for who adopts are proximity, income, age and personal networks. Getting animals seen in person shows large but observational associations. Engagement did not predict adoption. Displacement between animals is unmeasured. Most plans need paid reach that volunteers lack |

### Design changes that follow from the evidence

Reposition the product around what the evidence can carry. The honest value is that a volunteer gets a named, complete, accurate listing and a practical exposure checklist in a minute, aimed first at animals that have already waited. A simulation suggests promotion directed at slow-track animals yields more than promotion of animals that would leave in three days anyway ([Mavrovouniotis 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/16/8/1158)), and long-stayers are where the kennel-days are.

Treat the name as a do-no-harm feature. Generate several short, easily pronounced options and let the volunteer choose. Filter out villains, violent or tragic characters, weapon and aggression words, and living celebrities. Apply the strictest screening to bully-breed dogs, where the Ohio data show racially coded names carrying a 4.5-day penalty. Prefer references a broad local audience recognises over the highest over-indexed item, and check for duplicates so one show does not name five animals in the same shelter. Stop presenting the name as something that speeds adoption; its plausible job is memorability and shareability in a post, which belongs to promotion and is unmeasured.

Make the listing generator incapable of inventing facts. Use a structured input in which the volunteer records what was observed (behaviour with people, with other animals, on the lead, in the kennel) and marks everything else unknown, and constrain the model to restate only those facts. Never assert breed, "good with children", house-training or calmness unless the shelter has verified it; staff breed guesses are wrong for over 40% of dogs. Never soften or drop a bite history or severe guarding. Default to third person, plain language, behaviour toward people first, the present over the backstory, and roughly 75 to 150 words, on the understanding that the length target rests on blog-level data. Describe difficult behaviour plainly with a management suggestion instead of leading with prohibitions; that reconciliation of the UK and US practitioner positions is reasoned, not tested. Confine the cultural reference to the name and at most one line, and never let it imply temperament: a cat named after a show does not "love" that show's music. Require a volunteer to edit and approve the text, which addresses the specific reason readers discount AI copy. Stop generating invented backstories, emotive pity appeals and urgency language by default; none has outcome support and the first is the one documented complaint about the incumbent tool.

Do the minimum on photos and nothing with image generation. Prompt for one sharp photo with the face visible, which is the only attribute supported by converging evidence. Do not build features around backgrounds, props, people or poses, and do not alter the animal's image.

Rebuild the promotion plan around exposure the volunteer can deliver without an ad budget. Lead with the channels that have evidence: people near the shelter, the personal networks of foster carers and volunteers, in-person visibility (outings, events, an "Adopt Me" vest), and timing posts and events for Friday, Saturday and early evening. For New York dogs, include rescue partners and foster recruitment as audiences. Use taste data as a secondary, clearly experimental layer for choosing among local venues and community groups, and avoid explicit identity claims ("perfect for fans of…"). Add an equity check so the plan always includes at least one channel that reaches communities under-represented among adopters, including non-English channels where relevant, since default "most distinctive local tastes" will follow the majority or the most online group. Stop reporting likes, reach or clicks as success.

Build measurement in from the start, because this is where the tool can contribute something new. Log which variant each animal received and randomise at intake between a generic AI listing and a culturally tailored one, so volunteers do not pick the appealing animals. Make days from availability to adoption the primary outcome, analysed as time-to-event, with 30- and 90-day returns as safety outcomes and total shelter adoptions as the check on displacement. By the standard survival-analysis formula ([Schoenfeld 1983](https://doi.org/10.2307/2531021)), detecting a 50% faster adoption rate needs about 190 adoptions, 25% faster about 630, and 10% faster about 3,450, before allowing for clustering by shelter; a single small rescue cannot answer the question and a network could. A cheaper first test fits a hackathon: show local residents the "locally popular" items and measure recognition, then randomise the same animal photo with a generic versus a tailored name and blurb and measure click-to-enquire, labelled honestly as stated-interest evidence. Finally, make the output paste-ready for the description field of the shelter's existing software, since a separate website competes with "just ask ChatGPT".

### What you can honestly claim, and what to drop

You can say that the tool drafts a complete, plain-language listing, a set of name options and an exposure checklist in seconds from facts the volunteer enters; that its defaults follow the published research (third person, behaviour first, plain wording, no invented traits) while that research is observational; that it screens names for stereotype and negative associations; that it uses local cultural data to suggest names and venues as an experiment; that no existing tool was found combining these elements; and that it is built to measure whether any of this works, which nobody has done. You can cite the scale of the problem accurately: about 5.8 million animals entering US shelters and rescues in 2025, large dogs waiting about 70% longer than small dogs, and New York's main shelter system suspending intake when it passed 1,000 animals.

Avoid claiming that the tool gets animals adopted faster, shortens length of stay or saves lives; that names or culturally resonant names affect adoption; that AI-written bios raise adoption rates (only vendor marketing says so); that it "matches" animals to adopters or reaches "the right people" (area-level taste data cannot identify individuals, and no matching programme has controlled evidence of benefit); that engagement gains mean adoption gains; or that it saves shelters a dollar amount per day (published figures are average costs, not savings). Do not repeat the sector's unsourced statistics as evidence: "a great photo means adoption 29 days sooner" as a general rule, "up to 60% faster", "three times less likely without a photo", or anecdotes of the "Tipsy gets adopted over Bella" kind. The tagline promises the one mechanism with no evidence behind it; "every animal gets a good listing and gets seen" is what the evidence supports.

## What no study has answered, and what this review could not check

The open questions, in rough order of importance for your tool, are these. Does any choice of name causally change adoption speed, tested by random assignment? Do locally or culturally resonant names and copy beat competent generic copy on enquiries, adoption or returns? Does promoting one animal add an adoption or take one from a neighbour in the same building? Does the accuracy or tone of a listing change return rates? What share of adopters today choose a specific animal online before visiting, given that the 81% walk-in figure is from one shelter in 2012? Does choosing venues and groups by cultural taste outperform choosing them by proximity and pet interest? Do AI-written listings differ from human-written ones on any outcome, and do adopters notice or care? Do residents recognise the items an area-level taste index calls locally distinctive? How many adopters rename? Would randomly assigned better photos shorten stays? And how is variance in LOS divided between the animal, the listing, the shelter and chance? None of these has a study behind it, and your tool could produce the first evidence on the second, third and seventh.

This review has real limits. A shared web-search cap ran out part-way through every research strand, and PubMed, PubMed Central, Europe PMC, Taylor & Francis, several Shelter Animals Count PDFs, Qloo's documentation and vendor help centres were blocked or rate-limited. As a result many effect sizes come from abstracts, press releases or secondary summaries and are tagged as such; the adjacent marketing and psychology figures tagged *recalled* were not re-read; and figures extracted by automated page readers disagreed in at least three places (the Florida breed-label LOS change, the foster-ambassador days, and cells of the Hill's LOS chart). Forward-citation searches from the five papers you hold failed, so a recent replication of Rix or Nakamura could exist unseen.

"Nothing found" means two different things below. Searched and nothing found: randomised or locally resonant name studies; names against clicks; renaming rates; humour, urgency and restriction wording against outcomes; AI versus human listings; paid-ad experiments against adoptions; peer-reviewed TikTok or Instagram outcome studies; cat-café outcomes; controlled long-stay promotion; culturally tailored promotion with outcomes; a randomised matching trial; listing accuracy against returns; independent validation of Qloo. Not searched, because the budget ran out: managed intake, transport programmes, opening hours and cat training as adoption levers; Nextdoor and X; Google Ad Grants and programmatic advertising; mobile units and brewery, pizza-box, workplace and university partnerships; displacement and featured-listing studies; short-form video, livestreams and platform policies on AI-edited photos; and AI features inside the mainstream shelter systems. Those gaps are unranked, not ranked low. Three unread sources could change a conclusion: the New South Wales dog discount and LOS study, the full text of Lampe and Witte, and the full text of Reese 2021. Country coverage is narrow, with real-outcome evidence coming almost entirely from the US, UK, Australia, Canada, Czechia and Malaysia.

## Conclusion

The literature has measured at length what makes animals wait and has barely tested what a listing can do about it. That leaves your idea in an unusual position: the mechanism you built it around is unproven, and the evidence that would prove or sink it does not exist because nobody has had a tool that controls the name, the copy and the audience and records the outcome. The strongest version of this project is the one that says so. A tool that guarantees accuracy, covers every animal, points volunteers at in-person exposure and personal networks, and randomises its own cultural layer would be more honest than the incumbent bio generators and more useful to the field than another unevaluated one.

The evidence also changes where the value sits. For a kennelled dog in a walk-in municipal shelter, most adopters meet the animal by walking in and decide on how it behaves toward them; the listing barely enters. The listing matters most where there is no walk-in route, for foster-based rescues and animals placed off-site, where more than half of adopters arrive through the internet. That is the tool's natural niche, and in New York, where rescue partners are the largest single exit for the city shelter's dogs, it is a real one.
